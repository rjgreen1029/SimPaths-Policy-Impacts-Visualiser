Interpreting the Results

A short guide to reading microsimulation output correctly — what the numbers represent, how uncertainty is shown, and what assumptions are baked in

What is microsimulation?

SimPaths uses UK survey data (Understanding Society) to model how population level changes may occur over time. The model evolves a starting population forward year by year: ageing, education, employment, income, family formation, and health all update according to rules estimated from real survey data. For each year, the data is realigned to the expected distributions in the UK. Running the same starting population once to generate a prediction under the current rules (the Baseline) and once with a proposed change applied (the Scenario) can help us understand the potential effect of policy changes over time.

Because the whole population is simulated, the model can show outcomes for combinations of people (for example, single parents in the North East on benefits).

Using filters and highlighters

The filter tab will let you bring on and off groups from your plot, while the highlighter tab will let you keep specific groups in colour while greying out (but still showing) other groups.

Baseline vs. Scenario

Users can explore predictions for both baseline and policy scenarios through time series as line charts or barplots, highlighting or filtering by various groups. One feature of this dashboard involves visualising the difference between a predictions following a policy intervention and the baseline predictions. A positive value here indicates the policy produced a higher predicted mean or share than the baseline prediction.

Throughout the dashboard, Baseline and Scenario are always distinguished by:

Baseline — solid line / solid fill
Scenario — dashed line / hatched fill

Every figure shows a modelled estimate

These are simulated outputs, intended for research purposes of what may happen given the assumptions underlying the model. They are not official statistics. The model captures the mechanisms it was built to represent; it won't capture every real-world driver, and small changes in assumptions can shift results. Treat the dashboard as a tool for comparing the relative effect of a policy change against a like-for-like baseline, not as a statement about the future.

Uncertainty across runs

Individual life events (job loss, partnership, illness) are drawn probabilistically, so single trajectories are not necessarily meaningful. The model is run multiple times so that averages across runs can be computed, and the dashboard reports the mean across runs together with a 95% confidence interval.

On line charts, that interval is drawn as a soft shaded band sitting behind the lines. Use the small ▮ 95% CI toggle above any line chart to show or hide the bands; on bar charts, every tooltip reports a 95% CI: [lower, upper].

How to read the CI band width

A narrow band means the runs agreed closely — the run to run variation for that estimate is low. A wide band means the runs varied a lot at that point, so the estimate is less certain.

When an estimate is suppressed

If the underlying sample size within any contributing run drops below 10 — for example, a small population slice for a narrow age band in a small region — that estimate is too unreliable to show responsibly. Rather than plotting a noisy point, the dashboard suppresses it: on charts you'll see a gap in the line, or a small-multiple panel showing "Sample too small — suppressed" instead of a chart. This is a deliberate choice to avoid implying more precision than the underlying data supports.

Quick Guide

  • ✕ Don't read the dashboard as a forecast of what will happen — it's a comparison of two modelled scenarios .
  • ✕ Don't treat a suppressed (missing) estimate as "zero" or "no effect" — it means the sample was too small to say, not that nothing changed.
  • ✓ Do focus on the sign and rough size of the Baseline → Scenario difference, rather than either figure in isolation.
  • ✓ Do check the confidence interval before treating a difference between two groups, or two years, as reliable.
  • ✓ Do use the stratifiers (Age, Region, Income Quintile, etc.) to check whether an overall effect is broad-based or concentrated in a particular group.
