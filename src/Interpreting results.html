Guidance

Interpreting the Results

A short guide to reading microsimulation output correctly — what the numbers represent, how uncertainty is shown, and where a bit of care goes a long way.

What is microsimulation?

SimPaths builds a synthetic population of individuals and households that mirrors the UK population, then evolves that population forward year by year — ageing, education, employment, income, family formation, and health all update according to rules estimated from real survey data. Running the same starting population once with today's policy rules (the Baseline) and once with a proposed change applied (the Scenario) lets us isolate the effect of that one change, holding everything else constant.

Because the whole population is simulated, the model can show outcomes for combinations of people — for example, single parents in the North East on a specific benefit — that survey samples alone are often too small to say much about.

Baseline vs. Scenario — the comparison that matters

Most of the value in this dashboard isn't in either line on its own — it's in the gap between them. The Δ Baseline → Scenario view makes that gap the primary object on screen, plotting the modelled effect of the policy change directly, centred on zero.

Throughout the dashboard, Baseline and Scenario are always distinguished by the same visual channel, independent of colour, so the two stay distinguishable even in greyscale or for colourblind readers:

Baseline — solid line / solid fill
Scenario — dashed line / hatched fill

Every figure is a modelled estimate, not a forecast

These are simulated outputs, intended for research purposes — not predictions of what will actually happen, and not official statistics. The model captures the mechanisms it was built to represent; it won't capture every real-world driver, and small changes in assumptions can shift results. Treat the dashboard as a tool for comparing the relative effect of a policy change against a like-for-like baseline, not as a statement about the future.

Multiple runs, and what the shaded band means

Microsimulation has a random element built in — individual life events (job loss, partnership, illness) are drawn probabilistically, so any single run is noisy. To separate that noise from the underlying effect, the model is run multiple times, and the dashboard reports the mean across runs together with a 95% confidence interval, calculated as the mean ± 1.96 × the standard deviation across runs.

On line charts, that interval is drawn as a soft shaded band sitting behind the trajectory line — Baseline's band with a plain fill, Scenario's with a light dashed outline so overlapping bands stay tellable apart. Use the small ▮ 95% CI toggle above any line chart to show or hide the bands; on bar charts, the same interval appears as an error bar, and in every tooltip as a 95% CI: [lower, upper] line.

How to read the band width

A narrow band means the runs agreed closely — you can be fairly confident in that point. A wide band means the runs varied a lot at that point, so treat the estimate there more cautiously. If the Baseline and Scenario bands overlap substantially, the modelled difference between them at that point is less likely to be meaningful.

When an estimate is suppressed

If the underlying sample size within any contributing run drops below 100 — for example, a small population slice for a narrow age band in a small region — that estimate is too unreliable to show responsibly. Rather than plotting a noisy point, the dashboard suppresses it: on charts you'll see a gap in the line, or a small-multiple panel showing "Sample too small — suppressed" instead of a chart. This is a deliberate choice to avoid implying more precision than the underlying data supports.

Common pitfalls

  • ✕ Don't read the dashboard as a forecast of what will happen — it's a comparison of two modelled scenarios, not a prediction.
  • ✕ Don't over-interpret a single year-to-year wiggle in a line as meaningful on its own — check whether it sits comfortably inside the CI band.
  • ✕ Don't treat a suppressed (missing) estimate as "zero" or "no effect" — it means the sample was too small to say, not that nothing changed.
  • ✓ Do focus on the sign and rough size of the Baseline → Scenario difference, rather than either figure in isolation.
  • ✓ Do check the confidence interval before treating a difference between two groups, or two years, as reliable.
  • ✓ Do use the stratifiers (Age, Region, Income Quintile, etc.) to check whether an overall effect is broad-based or concentrated in a particular group.
